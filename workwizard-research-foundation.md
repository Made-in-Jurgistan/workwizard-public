## How to read this brief

This document pulls from several different kinds of material: educational research, government statistics, policy analysis, market data, and WorkWizard's own product design. Each answers a different question, and keeping them separate matters, because a market estimate and a peer-reviewed meta-analysis are not the same kind of evidence.

- **Official statistics** describe measured outcomes in a defined population. The share of students below a proficiency level is one example.
- **Peer-reviewed research** tests a relationship or intervention under a stated method, sample, and set of limits.
- **Policy reports** describe government priorities, rules, or system-level observations.
- **Market estimates** indicate commercial scale. They say nothing about whether a product helps anyone learn.
- **Product hypotheses** describe what WorkWizard is designed to do and the research mechanism behind each design choice. The pilot quantifies the realised effect.

A note on the statistical terms used throughout. *d*, *g*, and *r* are effect-size measures: they describe how large a difference or relationship is, independent of sample size. *OR* (odds ratio) describes how much more likely an outcome is in one group versus another. *SD* means standard deviation, a unit of spread used to compare results across different studies and scales. Where a finding is small (an effect size under roughly 0.2, for instance), we say so, rather than letting a striking percentage imply more than the underlying number supports.

When a source reports an *association* between two things, we describe it as an association, and we flag where the underlying study design supports a causal reading. Disengagement, motivation, attention, and learning loss are related but distinct terms, and we have tried to keep them separate rather than use them interchangeably.

## Part I — The opportunity: re-engaging a generation

### 1.1 The engagement crisis by the numbers

Student disengagement is not a matter of individual behaviour, parenting, or effort. Large-scale assessments show that it varies across countries, schools, subjects, and learners. That means it responds to structural conditions, and structural conditions can be changed. The data that measures the problem also identifies the lever: students who report having a voice in lesson topics report feeling more at home in school. The engagement gap is real, and it is closeable.

### 1.2 The attention economy context

Students are not simply bored. They are being actively competed for. TikTok, YouTube, and gaming platforms use behavioural psychology, algorithmic personalisation, and open-ended content loops engineered for retention, with measurable effects on adolescent attention. (*[DAK-Gesundheit, Mediensucht-Report 2024, Germany](https://www.dak.de/dak/unternehmen/reporte-forschung/dak-studie-mediensucht-2024_91442)*)

The OECD's *Students, Digital Devices and Success* report (May 2024) found that nearly one in three students is distracted by digital devices in class, and that the relationship between device use and learning outcomes turns negative after about one hour a day, even for educational use at school. (*[OECD, 2024](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2024/05/students-digital-devices-and-success_621829ff.html)*; *[EU NESET Report on Screen Time and Educational Outcomes, 2024](https://education-socioeconomic-experts.ec.europa.eu/document/download/4836f176-33dc-4d6b-b9c9-05fd2d1a384e_en)*)

Phones in class are often a **symptom** of lessons that lack relevance, not simply a classroom-management problem. OECD's PISA data link peer device distraction to measurable learning loss, which suggests the underlying driver is competition for attention rather than the devices themselves. (*[OECD, 2024](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2024/05/students-digital-devices-and-success_621829ff.html)*)

Most classrooms still offer a single, one-size-fits-all worksheet to every student. The gap between what holds a child's attention and what the school assigns is structural, not a matter of individual motivation.

### 1.3 The cognitive dimension

The engagement crisis is not only about motivation. Recent research also links heavy device use to measurable changes in cognitive capability itself.

**The reverse Flynn effect**

Through most of the 20th century, IQ scores rose by roughly 3 points per decade. Around 2000, that trend began to reverse in high-income countries. The clearest evidence comes from Norwegian conscription data: IQ peaked for the birth cohort of 1975 and has since fallen by about 0.2 points a year, roughly 2 points per decade. Because this decline appears *within* families, it points toward environmental rather than genetic or demographic explanations. (*[Bratsberg & Rogeberg, PNAS, 2018](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1718793115)*) Some broader analyses report steeper population-level declines. Horvath (2025) cites estimates of several points per decade in places, though the size varies by dataset and test. (*[Horvath, 2025](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/media/doc/Horvath_Written%20Testimony.pdf)*)

The decline is not uniform. Tasks that depend on internal, language-mediated cognition (memory, executive function) show meaningful declines, while tasks that rely on externally scaffolded skills, visual perception and pattern recognition, show moderate growth. This pattern is consistent with a generation raised on visually guided digital tools rather than a general loss of intelligence. (*["Digging In," APA PsycNet, June 2025](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2026-46387-001)*)

As neuroscientist Jared Cooney Horvath told the U.S. Senate: **"Our kids are less cognitively capable than we were at their age."** Gen Z is the first generation in modern testing history to score lower than the generation before it. IQ is best understood as a measure of "school-ability". Each additional year of formal education adds roughly 1 to 5 IQ points, so a generation spending more time in school while still losing ground is a notable reversal. (*[Horvath, 2025](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/media/doc/Horvath_Written%20Testimony.pdf)*; *[Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, Psychological Science, 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797618774258)*)

**AI tools and critical thinking**

A 2025 study of 666 UK adults (Gerlich, SBS Swiss Business School) found a strong negative correlation between AI tool usage and critical-thinking scores (r = −0.68). Cognitive offloading, letting a tool do the thinking, was strongly associated with AI use (r = +0.72) and inversely associated with critical thinking. Younger users, aged 17–25, showed both the highest AI dependence and the lowest critical-thinking scores. (*[Gerlich, Societies, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/15/1/6)*; DOI: 10.3390/soc15010006)

A 2026 meta-analysis of 72 studies (n = 71,633) found screen time significantly associated with reduced academic performance (OR = 1.79), with the risk rising sharply above 2.5 hours a day. (*[BMC Public Health, Springer Nature, March 2026](https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/)*) A separate cohort study of 3,322 Canadian children found that high screen time in early childhood was associated with lower reading and maths scores later in elementary school. (*[JAMA Network Open, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.37092)*)

As Horvath put it in [*The Digital Delusion* (2025)](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/838437/the-digital-delusion-by-jared-cooney-horvath-phd-med/), echoed on [CNN in June 2026](https://www.cnn.com/2026/06/01/health/screens-in-school-education-tech-wellness): **"Students who use computers more perform worse academically."** His broader point is about mechanism rather than devices per se: relying on easy digital shortcuts removes the productive friction that learning requires. **"Friction is not a flaw of learning: it is learning."**

> **Why this matters for product design.** These findings describe a loop. Disengagement leads to more screen-based "solutions," which encourage cognitive outsourcing, which weakens the skills needed to re-engage. WorkWizard's print-first architecture breaks that loop at the tool level. The AI does the preparation, the child does the thinking, and the screen never enters the moment where learning actually happens.

## Part II — The consequences

### 2.1 The disengagement feedback loop

Disengagement does not stay contained in the classroom. The evidence points to several related, but separately-evidenced, pathways through the education system and into the wider economy:

1. Student engagement is associated with classroom participation and behaviour.
2. Classroom behaviour and teacher working conditions are associated with teacher stress and retention.
3. Unequal access to support widens family financial pressure.
4. Unfinished learning is linked, in scenario models, to long-term economic costs.

These four links form the causal chain WorkWizard is designed to interrupt at its earliest point (student apathy), before the downstream costs compound. The strength of evidence differs at each step, but the direction is consistent across countries, populations, and outcomes measured.

Student disengagement drives lower participation, more disruptive behaviour, and greater strain on teachers. Pay, leadership, workload, and local labour markets all contribute to attrition, but the association between student behaviour and teacher departure is well documented and quantifiable, which makes it a lever WorkWizard can pull directly.

### 2.3 Parental stress and financial pressure

When schools cannot personalise instruction at scale, the cost of remediation often falls to parents: financially, emotionally, and in time.

**Financial pressure**

- Parents globally spend an estimated $70 billion a year on private tutoring — a direct commercial consequence of school-based disengagement. (*[Future Market Insights, 2025](https://www.futuremarketinsights.com/reports/private-tutoring-market)*)
- The European private tutoring market alone is valued at $10.35 billion (2025), roughly 15% of global revenue, and growing. (*[Fortune Business Insights, 2025](https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/private-tutoring-market-104753)*)
- UK A-level students who use private tutoring report mixed academic benefit, alongside consistent non-academic costs: reduced confidence, increased performance anxiety, and a weaker connection to the classroom. (*[Walker & Zhang, *Issues in Educational Research*, 2024](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/378778605_Private_Tutoring_and_its_Effect_on_Students'_Learning_Performance)*)

**The tutoring paradox**

South Korean elementary students who consistently attended private tutoring in fifth and sixth grade showed *higher*, not lower, behavioural disengagement back in the regular classroom. (*[Byun, *Comparative Education Review*, 2026](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/739181)*) This points to a difficult dynamic. Families spend more, and students disengage further in the classroom their tutoring is meant to support. The cycle repeats. Wealthier families can sustain it and lower-income families cannot, which widens the attainment gap.

Socioeconomic status accounts for more than 20% of maths-score variation in some OECD countries, and the families who can afford tutoring are rarely the families who need it most. (*[OECD, Education at a Glance 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/education-at-a-glance-2025_1c0d9c79-en.html)*)

The "forty minutes of arguing for fifteen minutes of homework" experience many parents describe is not anecdotal exaggeration. It is the lived result of school content that has no emotional connection to the child in front of it. The personalisation is what is failing here, not the parent.

### 2.5 The human signal

The statistics above describe a system-level pattern. The experience underneath them is personal and immediate, recognisable to any parent who has sat across the kitchen table from a child who has simply shut down.

> **Forty minutes of arguing for fifteen minutes of work.**

That ratio does not describe a lazy child. It describes a child who has lost the connection between effort and meaning. The assignment feels arbitrary. The context is abstract. The reward is invisible. And at ten years old, with a phone or console within reach, the child is making a perfectly rational cost-benefit calculation. The same child who won't touch the worksheet will spend three hours building a redstone contraption in Minecraft, because there the effort connects to something they care about.

The parent's experience mirrors it: frustration, guilt, and helplessness in roughly equal measure. Tutoring is expensive. Many educational apps add to the household's screen-time load rather than reducing it. Typing a homework question into a chatbot produces something that looks like an answer but often has the wrong tone, framing, or level for the actual assignment. The tools that exist tend to serve the curriculum, not the learner in front of it.

Teachers are not failing either. A class of 25 students represents 25 different motivational profiles and 25 different relationships to the subject matter. Typically, there is exactly one version of the worksheet. No teacher can hand-write 25 personalised versions before morning.

But every one of those 25 children has something they care about. The interest is already there. The worksheet has not met it yet.

Students, parents, and teachers converge on a common message: *something has to change, and the tools available either don't help or make things worse.* That signal is the demand WorkWizard is designed to meet, and the research in the next section shows why the existing category has left the gap open.

Most educational apps are themselves screen-based, and every additional login, feed, or session competes for a child's already limited attention. The educational value of that added screen time should be demonstrated, not assumed.

> **WorkWizard's design invariant.** AI runs once, before the child sees anything; the learning itself happens on paper. Screen time is *subtracted* from the process, not stacked on top of it.

**Risk two — shortening attention spans**

- Students who use devices for leisure more than one hour a day at school score lower in mathematics. (*[OECD PISA 2022 analysis, 2024](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2024/05/students-digital-devices-and-success_621829ff.html)*)

(*[Delgado et al., 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131518301052)*; *[Salmerón et al., 2024](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2024-16892-001)*; *[Van der Meer & Van der Weel, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945/full)*)
- Frequent task-switching is linked to weaker memory formation and more errors. In one university study, students were off-task roughly two-thirds of the time when using computers in lecture; a 2025 systematic review confirms digital distraction remains a major driver of poor performance and instruction across settings. (*[Ragan et al., 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131514001158)*; *[Martin et al., 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-025-10550-6)*)

**Risk three — competition that trains the wrong motivation**

- A meta-analysis of 128 studies found that engagement-contingent rewards undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40), and that the effect is more damaging for children than for college students. (*[Deci, Koestner & Ryan, *Psychological Bulletin*, 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)*)
- An updated synthesis of 142 randomised controlled trials (2023) found similar effects: engagement-contingent rewards at d = −0.42, completion-contingent rewards at d = −0.48, and positive feedback at +0.33, consistent with the original theory a quarter-century later. (*[Lehtivuori, University of Turku, 2023](https://www.utupub.fi/handle/10024/173853)*)
- In a classroom trial of badges and a leaderboard (n = 71), the gamified section showed *declining* intrinsic motivation, satisfaction, and sense of empowerment, along with lower final exam scores. (*[Hanus & Fox, *Computers & Education*, 2015](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Assessing-the-effects-of-gamification-in-the-A-on-Hanus-Fox/dff76a9862467d426113ec530f83942016ae3a97)*)
- This is not a blanket case against games. Some gamification meta-analyses report small positive average effects, depending on the mechanics used ([Sailer & Homner, 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w), g ≈ 0.36). WorkWizard's position is narrower than "games are bad". It avoids badge, streak, and point-based homework loops, and it does not require an extra screen session to "earn" engagement.
- Apps that optimise for time-on-app and return visits are, by design, working against the goal of getting a student back to the teacher's paper worksheet.

> **The underlying tension.** Screen use and reward mechanics can support some learning goals and undermine others. Their value depends on the specific mechanic, the learner, the task, and how it is implemented. That is exactly the design dimension WorkWizard has chosen to control for, by removing the screen and the reward loop from the learning task itself.

## Part IV — A research-informed product, built to move the lever

WorkWizard draws on a research base that has developed over decades across developmental psychology, cognitive science, and classroom practice. The mechanisms it uses (interest-based personalisation, student agency, intrinsic motivation, and an analogue completion format) are among the most extensively studied interventions in the educational literature, and each maps directly to a concrete product decision. The science is settled. The product is what brings it to scale.

Interest-aligned content is not just correlated with higher engagement in the literature. It is the intervention point that interrupts the disengagement feedback loop near its source, before burnout, financial pressure, and economic loss compound downstream. That is the mechanism this product is designed to operationalise, and the sections below show how each design choice connects to the evidence.

---

### 4.1 Interest-based personalisation

Research consistently associates content aligned with student interests with stronger engagement. In the literature, it is a more durable lever than extrinsic rewards, more scalable than one-on-one tutoring, and it does not require winning a direct competition with a student's phone. It works with the child's existing motivation rather than against it.

**Preserving curiosity**

German students report wanting more voice in lesson topics, and those who get it report feeling more at home in school. (*[Robert Bosch Stiftung, Deutsches Schulbarometer Schüler:innen 2025/26](https://www.bosch-stiftung.de/de/projekt/das-deutsche-schulbarometer/schuelerinnen)*) With only 37.6% of German 15-year-olds saying they love learning new things at school, the upside is enormous: more than six in ten students are reachable by an approach that reconnects the worksheet to something they already care about. Interest-aligned instruction is the structural fix with the largest reachable population, not a nice-to-have. (*[OECD PISA 2022 / Education GPS: Germany](https://gpseducation.oecd.org/CountryProfile?primaryCountry=DEU&treshold=10&topic=PI)*)

**Intrinsic motivation versus the gamification failure mode**

A meta-analysis of 128 studies found engagement-contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40), completion-contingent rewards do so slightly less (d = −0.36), and performance-contingent rewards least of all (d = −0.28), while positive feedback strengthens it (d = +0.33). (*[Deci, Koestner & Ryan, *Psychological Bulletin*, 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)*) Reward effects depend on contingency type, age, and context, which is why WorkWizard avoids coercive reward loops by design. The product uses no leaderboard, streak, or point mechanic. Its engagement comes from interest and choice. The literature identifies those as durable levers, and badge-streak-point loops as corrosive ones.

**Student agency as a policy-level consensus**

OECD's 2025 Education Policy Outlook reviews 230 policies across 35 education systems and treats learner agency, relevance, and participation as central policy levers. Those are the same levers WorkWizard operationalises in product. ([OECD Education Policy Outlook 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/education-policy-outlook-2025_c3f402ba-en.html)) PISA data cited in the same report note that intrinsic motivation is higher among students with more ambitious aspirations, and that agency-supportive instruction is a top predictor of mathematics performance in adolescence. The OECD summarises: *"Environments that make learning relevant, relational and purposeful, with effective teacher practices and quality teacher–student relationships, can sustain adolescents' motivation and self-belief, with lasting effects on lifelong learning."* Separately, EU research finds that inquiry-based and active teaching methods create conditions especially suited to working with students' existing interests. (*[European Journal of Psychology of Education, 2025](https://link.springer.com/journal/10212)*)

This body of evidence is what WorkWizard is built on. Relevance, agency, and meaningful participation are associated with engagement in the research literature, and WorkWizard operationalises each one in a concrete product feature. The IZA finding that classroom disruption predicts teacher departure identifies the downstream lever: reduce disengagement and the behavioural load on teachers falls with it. The pilot measures the intermediate outcomes (task initiation, completion, teacher-rated usefulness, classroom friction) that connect the mechanism to that downstream effect.

**A quantified link between interest and participation**

A multiple-regression study found a statistically significant positive relationship between interest plus learning motivation and student participation (r = 0.521, p < 0.01), with interest alone at r = 0.516 and motivation alone at r = 0.520. (*[Uyun et al., *Psikoislamedia: Jurnal Psikologi*, 2022](https://jurnal.ar-raniry.ac.id/index.php/Psikoislam/article/view/13794)*) This offers empirical support for the more basic claim underlying the whole hypothesis: interest-aligned content is associated with active participation, which is the behavioural precursor to learning.

Interest-based learning design typically rests on three principles: student-centred personalisation, real-world relevance, and student autonomy. Students working on topics they care about are believed to process and retain information more effectively, supporting better recall and transfer. (*[Sora Schools, 2024](https://soraschools.com/blog/key-benefits-of-interest-based-classes-in-online-education)*; [Hidi & Renninger four-phase model, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4)) Keller's ARCS model (Attention, Relevance, Confidence, Satisfaction) offers a useful design lens: interest-context framing aims at Attention, curriculum-native personalisation at Relevance, calibrated difficulty at Confidence, and intrinsic mastery at Satisfaction. (*[Keller, TU Delft OCW](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=ARCS+model+Keller)*)

A growth mindset (the belief that ability develops through effort) is widely considered core to sustained engagement, and teacher-parent collaboration helps make the messaging around it consistent. (*[Dweck, 2006](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/44330/mindset-by-carol-s-dweck-phd/)*; [teachers' growth-mindset meta-analysis, *Educational Psychology Review*, 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-024-09925-7); [Magrid Education, 2024](https://magrid.education/)) A robust moderated-mediation study published in August 2026 (n = 1,532 adolescents, socioeconomically diverse) found that **engagement, not mindset, is the primary mechanism** linking classroom quality to achievement in both maths and science, underscoring that engagement is the proximate lever, and mindset is one of several inputs that feed it. (*[Educational Researcher, August 2026](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312261435545)*)

### 4.2 Print and handwriting

Producing a printed, handwritten-response worksheet rather than an interactive app is a deliberate pedagogical and product choice, one that gives the child the format the evidence favours and removes the distractions the evidence warns against. The research base consistently finds advantages for print and handwriting on the tasks that matter for worksheet completion: reading comprehension, memory encoding, and sustained attention. WorkWizard is designed to deliver those advantages at scale, without asking a teacher to hand-set type or a child to fight their phone for focus.

| Evidence | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Meta-analysis, 54 studies | A modest paper advantage for reading comprehension (d = −0.21). | [Delgado et al., *Educational Research Review*, 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131518301052) |
| Update including handheld devices, 59 studies | A small but statistically significant print advantage (g ≈ −0.10 to −0.11), larger among undergraduates. | [Salmerón et al., *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 2024](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2024-16892-001) |
| UK Government review, 2025 | Consistent evidence for screen inferiority across age groups; young children in particular "may learn less well from screen-based media." | [UK Education Committee, *Screen Time Impacts on Education and Wellbeing*](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmeduc/915/report.html); [DfE expert panel report, 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/new-screen-time-guidance-for-parents-of-under-5s) |

> **Interpretation.** These studies report consistent advantages for print or handwriting on reading comprehension and memory-encoding outcomes. The effects vary by task, learner, text, and device. On the tasks WorkWizard targets (worksheet reading, handwritten response, sustained focus without notifications), the evidence points in the same direction. Print-first is a design choice grounded in that evidence, not a default.

## The bottom line

The engagement crisis is real, measurable, and structural, but it is not inevitable. The research that documents the problem also identifies the lever. When a worksheet speaks to a child's interests, that child engages. When the work happens on paper, the learning sticks. And when the teacher reviews the output before it reaches the student, the curriculum stays intact. None of these are new ideas. They are among the most studied findings in educational psychology. What is new is building a product that brings all three together, at scale, without adding screen time, teacher workload, or extrinsic reward pressure.

WorkWizard is that product. The pilot will quantify the gains. The research in this brief is why we expect them.
